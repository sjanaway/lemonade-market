A lemonade stall guide to prices

Why do some things cost more than others?

Nobody sits in an office deciding what everything should cost. Prices come from two groups of people pulling against each other: the people who want to buy something, and the people who make and sell it.

Step 1 · The buyers

Everyone has a “most I'd pay”

Imagine it's a summer's day and 120 people walk past your homemade lemonade stall. Each of them has a secret price in their head: the most they'd pay for a cup. Some are really thirsty and would pay £3. Some would only buy it if it were really cheap. Each person below stands for 5 people, and the number under them is their “most I'd pay”.

£1.50

The big idea: the higher the price, the fewer people buy. Economists call how much people want something demand.

Step 2 · The makers

Making lemonade isn't free

Now look at it from the other side. There are 15 lemonade stalls in town. Lemons, sugar and cups cost money, and some stalls are cheaper to run than others. A stall will only open if the price covers what it costs them to make a cup. Each stall makes 10 cups.

£1.50

The big idea: the higher the price, the more people want to make and sell it. Economists call this supply.

Step 3 · The market

Where buyers and makers meet

Put both groups together and something clever happens. If the price is too high, cups are left over and sellers have to drop their price. If it's too low, there's a queue and sellers realise they can charge more. The price gets pushed towards the spot where the lines cross.

Step 4 · Your turn

Run your own stall for a week

You start with £20. Each morning, check the weather and the price of lemons. Decide how many cups to make and what to charge. Cups you don't sell go warm and get thrown away, so don't make too many. Make too few and you'll miss out on customers.

Step 5 · The bigger picture

What's this got to do with how the country works?

The UK, like most countries, is mainly capitalist. That means most shops, farms and factories are owned by people and companies, not by the government. They decide what to make and what to charge. If they guess well, they make a profit. If they guess badly, they lose money, just like your stall.

Prices are messages

When lemonade got expensive in the heatwave, that told makers “make more!” and told buyers “maybe have water today”. Millions of prices send messages like this every day.

Profit is the reward, loss is the risk

People start businesses hoping to earn more than they spend. That's what pushes them to make things people want. But plenty of businesses lose money and close.

Competition keeps prices in check

On Sunday a new stall opened and you couldn't charge whatever you liked. When lots of businesses sell the same thing, they have to compete on price and quality.

Nobody is in charge

No single person knows how to make a pencil from scratch: the wood, the graphite, the paint, the metal bit. Thousands of people around the world each do their part because prices tell them it's worth it.

What markets are good at

  • Making lots of different things, cheaply
  • Reacting quickly when people want something new
  • Rewarding new ideas and hard work
  • Letting people choose what to buy

What markets aren't so good at

  • Looking after people who can't afford to pay
  • Protecting things nobody owns, like clean air and rivers
  • Things everyone needs but nobody would sell for profit, like street lights or the police
  • Stopping one big company getting too powerful

That's why every country mixes the two. Businesses run most things, and the government uses taxes to pay for the NHS, state schools, roads, bin collections and the police, and makes rules so markets play fair. People argue a lot about exactly how much of each there should be. That's a big part of what politics is about.

For talking about together

Questions to think about

  1. Why does a week in Cornwall cost so much more in the school holidays than in term time? Same cottage, same beach. What's different about how many families want to go?
  2. If every child suddenly wanted the same toy for Christmas, what would happen to its price? What would the toy company do next year? Think about the heatwave and what makers do when prices go up.
  3. Water is far more useful than diamonds. So why are diamonds so much more expensive? Useful isn't the same as rare. Most of us can get water from a tap.
  4. Why do Premier League footballers get paid so much more than nurses, even though nurses do really important work? How many people can play football at that level? How many people pay to watch? Is that fair? There's no single right answer.
  5. What are some important things that are free or cheap? Who pays for them? Libraries, parks, school, seeing a GP. They're paid for out of everyone's taxes, like income tax and VAT.
The numbers on this page are made up to keep things simple. Real markets are messier, but they follow the same pull between buyers and makers.
